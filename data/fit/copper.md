**Copper** is a [chemical element](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chemical_element "Chemical element"); it has [symbol](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chemical_symbol "Chemical symbol") **Cu** (from [Latin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin "Latin") _cuprum_[atomic number](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atomic_number "Atomic number") 29. It is a soft, malleable, and [ductile](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ductility "Ductility") metal with very high [thermal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thermal_conductivity "Thermal conductivity") and [electrical conductivity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electrical_conductivity "Electrical conductivity"). A freshly exposed surface of pure copper has a [pinkish-orange color](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Copper_\(color\) "Copper \(color\)"). Copper is used as a conductor of heat and electricity, as a [building material](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Building_material#Metal "Building material"), and as a constituent of various metal [alloys](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alloy "Alloy"), such as [sterling silver](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sterling_silver "Sterling silver") used in [jewelry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jewelry "Jewelry"), [cupronickel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cupronickel "Cupronickel") used to make marine hardware and [coins](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coin "Coin"), and [constantan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Constantan "Constantan") used in [strain gauges](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Strain_gauge "Strain gauge") and [thermocouples](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thermocouple "Thermocouple") for temperature measurement.
Copper is one of the few [native metals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Native_metal "Native metal"), meaning metals that occur naturally in a directly usable, unalloyed metallic form. This led to very early human use in several regions, from c.8000 BC. Thousands of years later, it was the first metal to be [smelted](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Smelting "Smelting") from sulfide ores, c.5000 BC; the first metal to be cast into a shape in a mold, c.4000 BC; and the first metal to be purposely alloyed with another metal, [tin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tin "Tin"), to create [bronze](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bronze "Bronze"), c.3500 BC.
Commonly encountered compounds are copper(II) salts, which often impart blue or green colors to such minerals as [azurite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Azurite "Azurite"), [malachite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Malachite "Malachite"), and [turquoise](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Turquoise "Turquoise"), and have been used widely and historically as [pigments](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pigment "Pigment").
Copper used in buildings, usually for roofing, oxidizes to form a green [patina](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Patina "Patina") of compounds called [verdigris](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Verdigris "Verdigris"). Copper is sometimes used in [decorative art](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Decorative_art "Decorative art"), both in its elemental metal form and in compounds as pigments. Copper compounds are used as [bacteriostatic agents](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bacteriostatic_agent "Bacteriostatic agent"), [fungicides](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fungicide "Fungicide"), and [wood preservatives](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wood_preservative "Wood preservative").
Copper is essential to all [aerobic organisms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aerobic_organism "Aerobic organism"). It is particularly associated with oxygen metabolism. For example, it is found in the respiratory enzyme complex [cytochrome c oxidase](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cytochrome_c_oxidase "Cytochrome c oxidase"), in the oxygen carrying [hemocyanin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hemocyanin "Hemocyanin"), and in several [hydroxylases](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hydroxylase "Hydroxylase"). Adult humans contain between 1.4 and 2.1mg of copper per kilogram of body weight.
## Etymology
The name of the metal derives from _aes cyprium_[Cyprus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cyprus "Cyprus")' in Latin. In [Late Latin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Late_Latin "Late Latin") this became _cuprum_ [Old English](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Old_English "Old English") adopted this as _coper_ _copper_ derives from that word and first used in the 12th century.
## Characteristics
A copper disc (99.95% pure) made by [continuous casting](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Continuous_casting "Continuous casting"); [etched](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Industrial_etching "Industrial etching") to reveal [crystallites](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crystallite "Crystallite") Copper just above its melting point keeps its pink luster color when enough light outshines the orange [incandescence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Incandescence "Incandescence") color.
### Physical
Copper, [silver](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Silver "Silver"), and [gold](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gold "Gold") are in [group 11](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Group_11_element "Group 11 element") of the periodic table; these three metals have one s-orbital electron on top of a filled d-[electron shell](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electron_shell "Electron shell") and are characterized by high [ductility](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ductility "Ductility") and electrical and thermal conductivity. The filled d-shells in these elements contribute little to interatomic interactions, which are dominated by the s-electrons through [metallic bonds](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metallic_bond "Metallic bond"). Unlike metals with incomplete d-shells, metallic bonds in copper are lacking a [covalent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Covalent_bond "Covalent bond") character and are relatively weak. This observation explains the low [hardness](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hardness "Hardness") and high ductility of [single crystals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monocrystalline "Monocrystalline") of copper. At the macroscopic scale, introduction of extended defects to the [crystal lattice](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crystal_lattice "Crystal lattice"), such as grain boundaries, hinders flow of the material under applied stress, thereby increasing its hardness. For this reason, copper is usually supplied in a fine-grained [polycrystalline](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polycrystalline "Polycrystalline") form, which has greater strength than monocrystalline forms.
The softness of copper partly explains its high electrical conductivity (59.6×106[S](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Siemens_\(unit\) "Siemens \(unit\)")/m) and high thermal conductivity, second highest (second only to silver) among pure metals at room temperature. This is because the resistivity to electron transport in metals at room temperature originates primarily from scattering of electrons on thermal vibrations of the lattice, which are relatively weak in a soft metal.
Copper is one of a few metallic elements with a natural color other than gray or silver. Pure copper is orange-red and acquires a reddish [tarnish](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tarnish "Tarnish") when exposed to air. This is due to the low [plasma frequency](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plasma_frequency "Plasma frequency") of the metal, which lies in the red part of the visible spectrum, causing it to absorb the higher-frequency green and blue colors.
As with other metals, if copper is put in contact with another metal in the presence of an [electrolyte](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electrolyte "Electrolyte"), [galvanic corrosion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Galvanic_corrosion "Galvanic corrosion") will occur.
### Chemical
Copper does not react with water, but it does slowly react with atmospheric oxygen to form a layer of brown-black [copper oxide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Copper_oxide "Copper oxide") which, unlike the [rust](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rust "Rust") that forms on iron in moist air, protects the underlying metal from further corrosion ([passivation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Passivation_\(chemistry\) "Passivation \(chemistry\)")). A green layer of [verdigris](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Verdigris "Verdigris") (copper carbonate) can often be seen on old copper structures, such as the roofing of many older buildings and the [Statue of Liberty](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Statue_of_Liberty "Statue of Liberty"). Copper [tarnishes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tarnish "Tarnish") when exposed to some [sulfur](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sulfur "Sulfur") compounds, with which it reacts to form various [copper sulfides](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Copper_sulfide "Copper sulfide").

### Occurrence
See also: [List of copper ores](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_copper_ores "List of copper ores")
Copper is produced in massive stars and is present in the Earth's crust in a proportion of about 50 [parts per million](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parts_per_million "Parts per million") (ppm). In nature, copper occurs in a variety of minerals, including [native copper](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Native_copper "Native copper"), copper sulfides such as [chalcopyrite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chalcopyrite "Chalcopyrite"), [bornite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bornite "Bornite"), [digenite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Digenite "Digenite"), [covellite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Covellite "Covellite"), and [chalcocite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chalcocite "Chalcocite"), copper [sulfosalts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sulfosalt_minerals "Sulfosalt minerals") such as [tetrahedite-tennantite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tetrahedrite "Tetrahedrite"), and [enargite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Enargite "Enargite"), copper carbonates such as [azurite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Azurite "Azurite") and [malachite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Malachite "Malachite"), and as copper(I) or copper(II) oxides such as [cuprite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cuprite "Cuprite") and [tenorite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tenorite "Tenorite"), respectively. The largest mass of elemental copper yet discovered weighed 420 tonnes and was found in 1857 on the [Keweenaw Peninsula](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Keweenaw_Peninsula "Keweenaw Peninsula") in Michigan, US. Native copper is a [polycrystal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polycrystal "Polycrystal"), with the largest single crystal ever described measuring 4.4 × 3.2 × 3.2 cm. Copper is the 26th most abundant element in [Earth's crust](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Earth's_crust "Earth's crust"), representing 50ppm compared with 75ppm for [zinc](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zinc "Zinc"), and 14ppm for [lead](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lead "Lead").
Typical background concentrations of copper do not exceed 1ng/m3 in the atmosphere; 150mg/kg in soil; 30mg/kg in vegetation; 2μg/L in freshwater and 0.5μg/L in seawater.
## Production
See also: [List of countries by copper production](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_countries_by_copper_production "List of countries by copper production")
[Chuquicamata](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chuquicamata "Chuquicamata"), in Chile, is one of the world's largest [open pit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Open-pit_mining "Open-pit mining") copper [mines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mining "Mining").
Most copper is mined or [extracted](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Copper_extraction_techniques "Copper extraction techniques") as copper sulfides from large [open pit mines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Open_pit_mine "Open pit mine") in [porphyry copper](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Porphyry_copper "Porphyry copper") deposits that contain 0.4 to 1.0% copper. Sites include [Chuquicamata](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chuquicamata "Chuquicamata"), in Chile, [Bingham Canyon Mine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bingham_Canyon_Mine "Bingham Canyon Mine"), in Utah, United States, and [El Chino Mine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/El_Chino_Mine "El Chino Mine"), in New Mexico, United States. According to the [British Geological Survey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/British_Geological_Survey "British Geological Survey"), in 2005, Chile was the top producer of copper with at least one-third of the world share followed by the United States, Indonesia and Peru. Chile, the world's largest copper producer, supplies the US with 70% of refined copper and alloy imports through 2024. Together with Canada (17%) and Peru (7%), they account for 94% of U.S. copper imports. Copper can also be recovered through the [in-situ leach](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/In-situ_leach "In-situ leach") process. Several sites in the state of Arizona are considered prime candidates for this method. The amount of copper in use is increasing and the quantity available is barely sufficient to allow all countries to reach developed world levels of usage. An alternative source of copper for [collection](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deep_sea_mining "Deep sea mining") currently being researched are [polymetallic nodules](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polymetallic_nodules "Polymetallic nodules"), which are located at the depths of the [Pacific Ocean](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pacific_Ocean "Pacific Ocean") approximately 3000–6500 meters below sea level. These nodules contain other valuable metals such as [cobalt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cobalt "Cobalt") and [nickel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nickel "Nickel").
### Reserves and prices
World production trend
Copper has been in use for at least 10,000 years, but more than 95% of all copper ever mined and [smelted](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Smelting "Smelting") has been extracted since 1900. As with many natural resources, the total amount of copper on Earth is vast, with around 1014 tons in the top kilometer of Earth's crust, which is about 5million years' worth at the current rate of extraction. However, only a tiny fraction of these reserves is economically viable with present-day prices and technologies. Estimates of copper reserves available for mining vary from 25 to 60 years, depending on core assumptions such as the growth rate. Recycling is a major source of copper in the modern world.
Price of Copper 1959–2022
The price of copper is [volatile](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Volatility_\(finance\) "Volatility \(finance\)"). After a peak in 2022 the price unexpectedly fell. And by May 2024, the price on the [London Metal Exchange](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/London_Metal_Exchange "London Metal Exchange") has reached an all-time high above $11,000 per ton.
The global market for copper is one of the most [commodified](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Commodification "Commodification") and [financialized](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Financialization "Financialization") of the [commodity markets](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Commodity_market "Commodity market"), and has been so for decades.:213 The top exporters of raw copper were Zambia ($6.95B) and Chile ($2.16B), followed by Democratic Republic of the Congo ($1.41B). Raw copper's top importers were China ($6.13B), Switzerland ($3.15B), and India ($2.05B).
### Copper demand
In 2024, global copper production was estimated at 22.8–22.9 million metric tons. Copper demand is increasing due to the ongoing [energy transition to electricity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electrification "Electrification"). China accounts for over half the demand.
Global demand for copper is projected to increase by 50% by the year 2040. This substantial growth is driven by the rapid expansion of [clean energy technologies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Clean_technology "Clean technology"), [electric vehicle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electric_vehicle "Electric vehicle") production, and widespread [electrical grid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electrical_grid "Electrical grid") infrastructure upgrades over the next two decades.
### Extraction
Main article: [Copper extraction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Copper_extraction "Copper extraction")
Scheme of flash smelting process
The great majority of copper ores are [sulfides](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sulfide "Sulfide"). Common ores are the sulfides [chalcopyrite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chalcopyrite "Chalcopyrite") (CuFeS2), [bornite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bornite "Bornite") (Cu5FeS4) and, to a lesser extent, [covellite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Covellite "Covellite") (CuS) and [chalcocite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chalcocite "Chalcocite") (Cu2S). These ores occur at the level of <1% Cu. Concentration of the ore is required, which begins with [comminution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Comminution "Comminution") followed by [froth flotation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Froth_flotation "Froth flotation"). The remaining concentrate is smelted, which can be described with two simplified equations:
  * Cuprous sulfide is oxidized to cuprous oxide:

    2 Cu2S + 3 O2 → 2 Cu2O + 2 SO2
  * Cuprous oxide reacts with cuprous sulfide to convert to _blister copper_ upon heating:

    2 Cu2O + Cu2S → 6 Cu + 2 SO2
This produces crude copper, which is about 98% copper by mass and is then purified by electrolysis to a purity of up to 99%. During the electrolysis small amounts of [silver](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Silver "Silver") and gold may be precipitated into a sludge that can be reprocessed to recover the precious metals.:798
Aside from sulfides, another family of ores are oxides. Approximately 15% of the world's copper supply derives from these oxides. The beneficiation process for oxides involves extraction with sulfuric acid solutions followed by electrolysis. In parallel with the above method for "concentrated" sulfide and oxide ores, copper is recovered from [mine tailings](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mine_tailing "Mine tailing") and heaps. A variety of methods are used including leaching with sulfuric acid, ammonia, ferric chloride. Biological methods are also used.
A potential source of copper is polymetallic nodules, which have an estimated concentration 1.3%.

### Recycling
According to the [International Resource Panel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/International_Resource_Panel "International Resource Panel")'s [Metal Stocks in Society report](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metal_Stocks_in_Society_report "Metal Stocks in Society report"), the global per capita stock of copper in use in society is 35–55kg. Much of this is in more-developed countries (140–300kg per capita) rather than less-developed countries (30–40kg per capita). In 2001, a typical automobile contained 20–30kg of copper. By 2014, the copper and copper alloy content of [internal combustion engine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internal_combustion_engine "Internal combustion engine") vehicles decreased to 16.8kg, but increased again to 24.5kg by 2023. At the same time, a battery [electric vehicle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electric_vehicle "Electric vehicle") already contains around 91kg of copper and copper alloys.
Like [aluminium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aluminium "Aluminium"), copper is recyclable without any loss of quality, both from raw state and from manufactured products. An estimated 80% of all copper ever mined is still in use today. In volume, copper is the third most recycled metal after iron and aluminium. As of 2023[[update]](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Copper&action=edit) of global demand.
The process of recycling copper is roughly the same as is used to extract copper but requires fewer steps. High-purity scrap copper is melted in a [furnace](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metallurgical_furnace "Metallurgical furnace") and then [reduced](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Redox "Redox") and cast into [billets](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Billet_\(semi-finished_product\) "Billet \(semi-finished product\)") and [ingots](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ingot "Ingot"). Lower-purity scrap is melted to form _black copper_ (70–90% pure, containing impurities such as iron, zinc, tin, and nickel), followed by oxidation of impurities in a [converter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Converting_\(metallurgy\) "Converting \(metallurgy\)") to form blister copper (96–98% pure), which is then [refined](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Copper_extraction#Refining "Copper extraction") as before.:202
### Environmental impacts
[Acid mine drainage](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Acid_mine_drainage "Acid mine drainage") from the disused [Parys Mountain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parys_Mountain "Parys Mountain") copper mines
Copper mining is energy intensive. The extraction generates a substantial waste stream, including potentially toxic dust and heavy metal contamination near sites where it is refined. Since most copper ores are sulfides, smelting is required. Smelting generates [sulfur dioxide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sulfur_dioxide "Sulfur dioxide"), a precursor to atmospheric [sulfuric acid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sulfuric_acid "Sulfuric acid"), unless it is captured. About 15% of copper comes from low grade (dilute) ore bodies. Processing is achieved by [hydrometallurgy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hydrometallurgy "Hydrometallurgy"), requiring acidic extractants, which remains in the waste streams after electrolytic deposition of the copper.
## Alloys
Copper alloys are widely used in the production of coinage; seen here are two examples – post-1964 American [dimes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dime_\(United_States_coin\) "Dime \(United States coin\)"), which are composed of the alloy [cupronickel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cupronickel "Cupronickel") and a pre-1968 [Canadian dime](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dime_\(Canadian_coin\) "Dime \(Canadian coin\)"), which is composed of an alloy of 80 percent silver and 20 percent copper.
See also: [List of copper alloys](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_copper_alloys "List of copper alloys")
Numerous copper [alloys](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alloy "Alloy") have been formulated, many with important uses. [Brass](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brass "Brass") is an alloy of copper and [zinc](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zinc "Zinc"). [Bronze](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bronze "Bronze") usually refers to copper–[tin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tin "Tin") alloys, but can refer to any alloy of copper such as [aluminium bronze](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aluminium_bronze "Aluminium bronze"). Copper-tin bronzes with various additional metals have been used to create [bells](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bell "Bell") for centuries; the composition of the alloy directly affects the tone and mechanical characteristics of the instrument. Copper is one of the most important constituents of silver and [karat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fineness "Fineness") gold solders used in the jewelry industry, modifying the color, hardness and melting point of the resulting alloys. Some lead-free [solders](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solder#Solder_alloys "Solder") consist of tin alloyed with a small proportion of copper and other metals.
The alloy of copper and [nickel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nickel "Nickel"), called [cupronickel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cupronickel "Cupronickel"), is used in low-denomination coins, often for the outer cladding. The US five-cent coin (currently called a _nickel_) consists of 75% copper and 25% nickel in homogeneous composition. Prior to the introduction of cupronickel, which was widely adopted by countries in the latter half of the 20th century, alloys of copper and [silver](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Silver "Silver") were also used, with the United States using an alloy of 90% silver and 10% copper until 1965, when circulating silver was removed from all coins with the exception of the half dollar—these were debased to an alloy of 40% silver and 60% copper between 1965 and 1970. The alloy of 90% copper and 10% nickel, remarkable for its resistance to corrosion, is used for various objects exposed to seawater, though it is vulnerable to the sulfides sometimes found in polluted harbors and estuaries. Alloys of copper with aluminium (about 7%) have a golden color and are used in decorations. is a Japanese decorative alloy of copper containing a low percentage of gold, typically 4–10%, that can be [patinated](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Patina "Patina") to a dark blue or black color.
## Compounds
A sample of [copper(I) oxide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Copper\(I\)_oxide "Copper\(I\) oxide")
Main article: [Copper compounds](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Copper_compounds "Copper compounds")
Copper forms a rich variety of compounds, usually with [oxidation states](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oxidation_state "Oxidation state") +1 and +2, which are often called _cuprous_ and _cupric_ , respectively. Copper compounds promote or catalyse numerous chemical and biological processes.
### Binary compounds
As with other elements, the simplest compounds of copper are binary compounds, i.e. those containing only two elements, the principal examples being oxides, sulfides, and [halides](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Halide "Halide"). Both [cuprous](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Copper\(I\)_oxide "Copper\(I\) oxide") and [cupric oxides](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Copper\(II\)_oxide "Copper\(II\) oxide") are known. Numerous [copper sulfides](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Copper_sulfide "Copper sulfide") are known, being mainly of interest as ores. The stoichiometrically simplest examples [copper(I) sulfide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Copper\(I\)_sulfide "Copper\(I\) sulfide") () and [copper monosulfide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Copper_monosulfide "Copper monosulfide") (CuS).
Cuprous [chloride](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Copper\(I\)_chloride "Copper\(I\) chloride"), [bromide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Copper\(I\)_bromide "Copper\(I\) bromide"), and [iodide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Copper\(I\)_iodide "Copper\(I\) iodide") are known, but [copper(I) fluoride](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Copper\(I\)_fluoride "Copper\(I\) fluoride") remains elusive. Cupric [fluoride](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Copper\(II\)_fluoride "Copper\(II\) fluoride"), [chloride](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Copper\(II\)_chloride "Copper\(II\) chloride"), and [bromide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Copper\(II\)_bromide "Copper\(II\) bromide") are also well characterized. Attempts to prepare copper(II) iodide yield only copper(I) iodide and iodine.     2 Cu2+ + 4 I− → 2 CuI + I2
### Coordination chemistry
Copper(II) gives a deep blue coloration in the presence of ammonia ligands. The one used here is [tetraamminecopper(II) sulfate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tetraamminecopper\(II\)_sulfate "Tetraamminecopper\(II\) sulfate").
Copper forms [coordination complexes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coordination_complex "Coordination complex") with [ligands](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ligand "Ligand"). In [aqueous solution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aqueous_solution "Aqueous solution"), copper(II) exists as [Cu(HO)]. This complex exhibits the fastest water exchange rate (speed of water ligands attaching and detaching) for any transition [metal aquo complex](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metal_aquo_complex "Metal aquo complex"). Adding aqueous [sodium hydroxide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sodium_hydroxide "Sodium hydroxide") causes the precipitation of light blue solid [copper(II) hydroxide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Copper\(II\)_hydroxide "Copper\(II\) hydroxide"). A simplified equation is: 
Pourbaix diagram for copper in uncomplexed media (anions other than OH- not considered). Ion concentration 0.001m (mol/kg water). Temperature 25°C.     Cu2+ + 2 OH− → Cu(OH)2
[Aqueous ammonia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ammonia_solution "Ammonia solution") results in the same precipitate. Upon adding excess ammonia, the precipitate dissolves, forming [tetraamminecopper(II)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Schweizer's_reagent "Schweizer's reagent"):      Cu(HO)(OH) + 4 NH3 → [Cu(HO)(NH)] + 2 H2O + 2 OH−
Many other [oxyanions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oxyanion "Oxyanion") form complexes; these include [copper(II) acetate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Copper\(II\)_acetate "Copper\(II\) acetate"), [copper(II) nitrate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Copper\(II\)_nitrate "Copper\(II\) nitrate"), and [copper(II) carbonate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Copper\(II\)_carbonate "Copper\(II\) carbonate"). [Copper(II) sulfate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Copper\(II\)_sulfate "Copper\(II\) sulfate") forms a blue crystalline penta[hydrate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hydrate "Hydrate"), the most familiar copper compound in the laboratory. It is used in a [fungicide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fungicide "Fungicide") called the [Bordeaux mixture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bordeaux_mixture "Bordeaux mixture").
[Ball-and-stick model](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ball-and-stick_model "Ball-and-stick model") of the complex [Cu(NH3)4(H2O)2]2+, illustrating the [octahedral coordination geometry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Octahedral_coordination_geometry "Octahedral coordination geometry") common for copper(II)
[Polyols](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polyol "Polyol"), compounds containing more than one alcohol [functional group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Functional_group "Functional group"), generally interact with cupric salts. For example, copper salts are used to test for [reducing sugars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reducing_sugars "Reducing sugars"). Specifically, using [Benedict's reagent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Benedict's_reagent "Benedict's reagent") and [Fehling's solution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fehling's_solution "Fehling's solution") the presence of the sugar is signaled by a color change from blue Cu(II) to reddish copper(I) oxide. Schweizer's reagent and related complexes with [ethylenediamine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ethylenediamine "Ethylenediamine") and other [amines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amine "Amine") dissolve [cellulose](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cellulose "Cellulose"). [Amino acids](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amino_acid "Amino acid") form very stable [chelate complexes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chelate_complex "Chelate complex") with copper(II).
### Organocopper chemistry
Main article: [Organocopper compound](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Organocopper_compound "Organocopper compound")
Compounds that contain a carbon-copper bond are known as organocopper compounds. They are very reactive towards oxygen to form copper(I) oxide and have [many uses in chemistry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reactions_of_organocopper_reagents "Reactions of organocopper reagents"). They are synthesized by treating copper(I) compounds with [Grignard reagents](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grignard_reaction "Grignard reaction"), [terminal alkynes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Terminal_alkyne "Terminal alkyne") or [organolithium reagents](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Organolithium_compound "Organolithium compound"); in particular, the last reaction described produces a [Gilman reagent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gilman_reagent "Gilman reagent"). These can undergo [substitution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Substitution_reaction "Substitution reaction") with [alkyl halides](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alkyl_halides "Alkyl halides") to form [coupling products](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coupling_reaction "Coupling reaction"); as such, they are important in the field of [organic synthesis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Organic_synthesis "Organic synthesis"). [Copper(I) acetylide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Copper\(I\)_acetylide "Copper\(I\) acetylide") is highly shock-sensitive but is an intermediate in reactions such as the [Cadiot–Chodkiewicz coupling](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cadiot%E2%80%93Chodkiewicz_coupling "Cadiot–Chodkiewicz coupling") and the [Sonogashira coupling](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sonogashira_coupling "Sonogashira coupling"). [Conjugate addition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nucleophilic_conjugate_addition "Nucleophilic conjugate addition") to [enones](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alpha-beta_Unsaturated_carbonyl_compounds "Alpha-beta Unsaturated carbonyl compounds") and [carbocupration](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carbometalation "Carbometalation") of alkynes can also be achieved with organocopper compounds. Copper(I) forms a variety of weak complexes with [alkenes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alkene "Alkene") and [carbon monoxide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carbon_monoxide "Carbon monoxide"), especially in the presence of amine ligands.
### Copper(III) and copper(IV)
Copper(III) is most often found in oxides. A simple example is potassium [cuprate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cuprate "Cuprate"), KCuO2, a blue-black solid. The most extensively studied copper(III) compounds are the [cuprate superconductors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cuprate_superconductor "Cuprate superconductor"). [Yttrium barium copper oxide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yttrium_barium_copper_oxide "Yttrium barium copper oxide") (YBa2Cu3O7) consists of both Cu(II) and Cu(III) centres. Like oxide, [fluoride](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fluoride "Fluoride") is a highly [basic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Base_\(chemistry\) "Base \(chemistry\)") [anion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anion "Anion") and is known to stabilize metal ions in high oxidation states. Both copper(III) and even copper(IV) fluorides are known, [K3CuF6](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Potassium_hexafluorocuprate\(III\) "Potassium hexafluorocuprate\(III\)") and [Cs2CuF6](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Caesium_hexafluorocuprate\(IV\) "Caesium hexafluorocuprate\(IV\)"), respectively.
Some copper proteins form [oxo complexes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oxo_complex "Oxo complex"), which, in extensively studied synthetic analog systems, feature copper(III). With [tetrapeptides](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tetrapeptide "Tetrapeptide"), purple-colored copper(III) complexes are stabilized by the deprotonated [amide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amide "Amide") ligands.
Complexes of copper(III) are also found as intermediates in reactions of organocopper compounds, for example in the [Kharasch–Sosnovsky reaction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kharasch%E2%80%93Sosnovsky_reaction "Kharasch–Sosnovsky reaction").
## Biological role
Main article: [Copper in biology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Copper_in_biology "Copper in biology")
### Biochemistry
Photosynthesis functions by an elaborate electron transport chain within the [thylakoid membrane](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thylakoid_membrane "Thylakoid membrane"). A central link in this chain is [plastocyanin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plastocyanin "Plastocyanin"), a blue copper protein.
The biological role for copper commenced with the appearance of oxygen in Earth's atmosphere.[](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Copper#cite_note-100) [Copper proteins](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Copper_proteins "Copper proteins") have diverse roles in biological electron transport and oxygen transportation, processes that exploit the easy interconversion of Cu(I) and Cu(II).[](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Copper#cite_note-101) Copper is essential in the aerobic [respiration](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cellular_respiration "Cellular respiration") of all [eukaryotes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eukaryotes "Eukaryotes"). In [mitochondria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mitochondria "Mitochondria"), it is found in [cytochrome c oxidase](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cytochrome_c_oxidase "Cytochrome c oxidase"), which is the last protein in [oxidative phosphorylation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oxidative_phosphorylation "Oxidative phosphorylation") which stores energy in [ATP](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adenosine_triphosphate "Adenosine triphosphate"). The copper atoms are alternatively reduced and oxidized during the electron transfer to oxygen.[](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Copper#cite_note-102):383 Copper is also found in many [superoxide dismutases](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Superoxide_dismutase "Superoxide dismutase"), proteins that catalyze the decomposition of [superoxides](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Superoxide "Superoxide") by converting it (by [disproportionation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Disproportionation "Disproportionation")) to oxygen and [hydrogen peroxide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hydrogen_peroxide "Hydrogen peroxide"):
  * Cu2+-SOD + O2− → Cu+-SOD + O2 (reduction of copper; oxidation of superoxide)
  * Cu+-SOD + O2− + 2H+ → Cu2+-SOD + H2O2 (oxidation of copper; reduction of superoxide)


The protein [hemocyanin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hemocyanin "Hemocyanin") is the oxygen carrier in most [mollusks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mollusk "Mollusk") and some [arthropods](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arthropod "Arthropod") such as the [horseshoe crab](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Horseshoe_crab "Horseshoe crab") (_Limulus polyphemus_).[](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Copper#cite_note-NOAA-103) Because hemocyanin is blue, these organisms have blue blood rather than the red blood of iron-based [hemoglobin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hemoglobin "Hemoglobin"). Structurally related to hemocyanin are the [laccases](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Laccase "Laccase") and [tyrosinases](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tyrosinase "Tyrosinase"). Instead of reversibly binding oxygen, these proteins hydroxylate substrates, illustrated by their role in the formation of [lacquers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lacquer "Lacquer").[](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Copper#cite_note-Lippard-104)
Several copper proteins, such as the [blue copper proteins](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Blue_copper_protein "Blue copper protein"), do not interact directly with substrates; hence they are not enzymes. These proteins relay electrons by the process called [electron transfer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electron_transfer "Electron transfer").[](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Copper#cite_note-Lippard-104) A unique tetranuclear copper center has been found in [nitrous-oxide reductase](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nitrous-oxide_reductase "Nitrous-oxide reductase").[](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Copper#cite_note-105)
Copper levels are closely regulated in both [prokaryotic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prokaryotic "Prokaryotic") and [eukaryotic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eukaryotic "Eukaryotic") cells to balance critical physiological need but avoid toxicity.[]
### Nutrition
Rich sources of copper include oysters, beef and lamb liver, Brazil nuts, blackstrap molasses, cocoa, and black pepper. Good sources include lobster, nuts and sunflower seeds, green olives, avocados, and wheat bran.
Copper is an essential [trace element](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trace_element "Trace element") in plants and animals, but not all microorganisms. The human body contains copper at a level of about 1.4 to 2.1mg per kg of body mass.[](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Copper#cite_note-copper.org-106)
#### Absorption and regulation
Copper is absorbed in the gut, then transported to the liver bound to [albumin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Serum_albumin "Serum albumin").[](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Copper#cite_note-107) After processing in the liver, copper is distributed to other tissues in a second phase, which involves the protein [ceruloplasmin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ceruloplasmin "Ceruloplasmin"), carrying the majority of copper in blood. Ceruloplasmin also carries the copper that is excreted in milk, and is particularly well-absorbed as a copper source.[](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Copper#cite_note-108) Copper in the body normally undergoes [enterohepatic circulation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Enterohepatic_circulation "Enterohepatic circulation") (about 5mg a day, vs. about 1mg per day absorbed in the diet and excreted from the body), and the body is able to excrete some excess copper, if needed, via [bile](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bile "Bile"), which carries some copper out of the liver that is not then reabsorbed by the intestine.[](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Copper#cite_note-109)[](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Copper#cite_note-110)
Copper levels and transport are tightly regulated processes. At least two copper-specific [chaperones](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chaperone_\(protein\) "Chaperone \(protein\)") have been isolated.[](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Copper#cite_note-111)
#### Dietary recommendations
The [U.S. Institute of Medicine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/U.S._Institute_of_Medicine "U.S. Institute of Medicine") updated the estimated average requirements (EARs) and recommended dietary allowances (RDAs) for copper in 2001. If there is not sufficient information to establish EARs and RDAs, an estimate designated [Adequate Intake](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adequate_Intake "Adequate Intake") (AI) is used instead. The AIs for copper are: 200μg of copper for 0–6-month-old males and females, and 220μg of copper for 7–12-month-old males and females. For both sexes, the RDAs for copper are: 340μg of copper for 1–3 years old, 440μg of copper for 4–8 years old, 700μg of copper for 9–13 years old, 890μg of copper for 14–18 years old and 900μg of copper for ages 19 years and older. For pregnancy, 1,000μg. For lactation, 1,300μg.[](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Copper#cite_note-112) As for safety, the Institute of Medicine also sets [tolerable upper intake levels](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tolerable_upper_intake_level "Tolerable upper intake level") (ULs) for vitamins and minerals when evidence is sufficient. In the case of copper, the UL is set at 10mg/day. Collectively the EARs, RDAs, AIs and ULs are referred to as [Dietary Reference Intakes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dietary_Reference_Intake "Dietary Reference Intake").[](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Copper#cite_note-113)
The [European Food Safety Authority](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/European_Food_Safety_Authority "European Food Safety Authority") (EFSA) refers to the collective set of information as Dietary Reference Values, with Population Reference Intake (PRI) instead of RDA, and Average Requirement instead of EAR. AI and UL are defined the same as in the United States. For women and men ages 18 and older, the AIs are set at 1.3 and 1.6mg/day, respectively. AIs for pregnancy and lactation is 1.5mg/day. For children ages 1–17 years, the AIs increase with age from 0.7 to 1.3mg/day. These AIs are higher than the U.S. RDAs.[](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Copper#cite_note-114) The European Food Safety Authority reviewed the same safety question and set its UL at 5mg/day, which is half the U.S. value.[](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Copper#cite_note-115)
For U.S. food and [dietary supplement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dietary_supplement "Dietary supplement") labeling purposes, the amount in a serving is expressed as a percent of Daily Value (%DV). In 2019, 100% of the Daily Value was revised to 0.9mg to bring it into agreement with the RDA.[](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Copper#cite_note-FedReg-116)[](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Copper#cite_note-117)
#### Deficiency
Main article: [Copper deficiency](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Copper_deficiency "Copper deficiency")
Because of its role in facilitating iron uptake, [copper deficiency](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Copper_deficiency "Copper deficiency") can produce [anemia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anemia "Anemia")-like symptoms, [neutropenia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neutropenia "Neutropenia"), bone abnormalities, hypopigmentation, impaired growth, increased incidence of infections, osteoporosis, hyperthyroidism, and abnormalities in glucose and cholesterol metabolism.[](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Copper#cite_note-118) Conversely, [Wilson's disease](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wilson's_disease "Wilson's disease") is genetic disease that causes an accumulation of copper in body tissues.[](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Copper#cite_note-119)
A minimum dietary value for healthy growth in [European rabbits](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/European_rabbit "European rabbit") has been reported to be at least 3[ppm](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parts_per_million "Parts per million") in the diet.[](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Copper#cite_note-120) However, higher concentrations of copper (100ppm, 200ppm, or 500ppm) in the diet of rabbits may favorably influence [feed conversion efficiency](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Feed_conversion_ratio "Feed conversion ratio"), growth rates, and carcass dressing percentages.[](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Copper#cite_note-121)
Severe deficiency can be found by testing for low plasma or serum copper levels, low ceruloplasmin, and low red blood cell superoxide dismutase levels; these are not sensitive to marginal copper status. The "cytochrome c oxidase activity of leucocytes and platelets" has been stated as another factor in deficiency, but the results have not been confirmed by replication.[](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Copper#cite_note-Bonhametal2002-122)
### Toxicity
Main article: [Copper toxicity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Copper_toxicity "Copper toxicity")
Chronic copper toxicity does not normally occur in humans because of transport systems that regulate absorption and excretion. No retention of copper is expected to occur at the 5mg/day level.[](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Copper#cite_note-123)
Research has shown a link between copper level regulation in the body and several neurological diseases, especially [Alzheimer's disease](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alzheimer's_disease "Alzheimer's disease"). The studies suggest the issue is an age-related breakdown of internal regulation mechanism rather than an exposure toxicity.[](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Copper#cite_note-124)
Gram quantities of various copper salts have been taken in suicide attempts and produced acute copper toxicity in humans, resulting in irreversible liver failure.[](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Copper#cite_note-Sailer-2024-125) Autosomal recessive mutations in copper transport proteins also cause regulation failure, leading to [Wilson's disease](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wilson's_disease "Wilson's disease") with copper accumulation, [cirrhosis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cirrhosis "Cirrhosis") of the liver, and psychiatric symptoms.[](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Copper#cite_note-Sailer-2024-125)
### Bioleaching
Bacteria have been used to extract copper in industrial scale [bioleaching](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bioleaching "Bioleaching") systems. Microbe solutions percolate through crushed ore converting insoluble copper sulfides to soluble copper sulfates; [electrowinning](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electrowinning "Electrowinning") extracts high purity copper from the resulting solution.[](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Copper#cite_note-Geoffrey_Michael_Gadd_609%E2%80%93643-126):628
### Human exposure
In the US, the [Occupational Safety and Health Administration](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Occupational_Safety_and_Health_Administration "Occupational Safety and Health Administration") (OSHA) has designated a [permissible exposure limit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Permissible_exposure_limit "Permissible exposure limit") (PEL) for copper dust in the workplace as a time-weighted average (TWA) of 1mg/m3 and 0.1mg/m3 for copper fumes[](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Copper#cite_note-127) The [National Institute for Occupational Safety and Health](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Institute_for_Occupational_Safety_and_Health "National Institute for Occupational Safety and Health") (NIOSH) has set a [recommended exposure limit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Recommended_exposure_limit "Recommended exposure limit") (REL) of 1mg/m3, time-weighted average. The [IDLH](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/IDLH "IDLH") (immediately dangerous to life and health) value is 100mg/m3.[](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Copper#cite_note-128) Copper is a constituent of [tobacco smoke](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tobacco_smoke "Tobacco smoke").[](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Copper#cite_note-129)[](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Copper#cite_note-TalhoutSchulz2011-130)
## History
### Prehistoric
#### Copper Age
Main article: [Copper Age](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Copper_Age "Copper Age")
A corroded copper [ingot](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ingot "Ingot") from [Zakros](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zakros "Zakros"), [Crete](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crete "Crete"), shaped in the form of an animal skin ([oxhide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oxhide_ingot "Oxhide ingot")) typical in that era
Copper ore ([chrysocolla](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chrysocolla "Chrysocolla")) in [Cambrian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cambrian "Cambrian") sandstone from [Chalcolithic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chalcolithic "Chalcolithic") mines in the [Timna Valley](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Timna_Valley "Timna Valley"), southern [Israel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Israel "Israel")
Copper artifacts from the [Old Copper Complex](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Old_Copper_Complex "Old Copper Complex") of North America, ~9500–5400 years before present
Copper occurs naturally as [native metallic copper](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Native_copper "Native copper") and was known to some of the oldest civilizations on record. The history of native copper use dates to 9000BC in the Middle East;[](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Copper#cite_note-discovery-131) a native copper pendant was found in northern Iraq that dates to 8700BC.[](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Copper#cite_note-132) Evidence suggests that gold and native [meteoric iron](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Meteoric_iron "Meteoric iron") were the only metals used by humans before copper and hot metallurgy.[](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Copper#cite_note-vander-133) The history of copper metallurgy is thought to follow this sequence: first came [cold working](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cold_forming "Cold forming") of native copper, then [annealing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Annealing_\(metallurgy\) "Annealing \(metallurgy\)"), followed by [smelting](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Smelting "Smelting"), and finally [casting](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Casting "Casting").[]
The earliest archaeological support of hot metallurgy in Eurasia is found in the [Balkans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Balkans "Balkans") and [Carpathian Mountains](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carpathian_Mountains "Carpathian Mountains") as evidenced by findings of copper objects made by [metal casting](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metal_casting "Metal casting") and [smelting](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Smelting "Smelting") dated to between 6200 and 5000 BC.[](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Copper#cite_note-134)[](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Copper#cite_note-:1-135)[](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Copper#cite_note-:0-136)[](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Copper#cite_note-Chernykh-2014-137) The earliest example of [lost-wax cast](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lost-wax_casting "Lost-wax casting") copper comes from an amulet found in [Mehrgarh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mehrgarh "Mehrgarh"), Pakistan dated to 4000BC.[](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Copper#cite_note-138) [Ötzi the Iceman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/%C3%96tzi_the_Iceman "Ötzi the Iceman"), a male dated from 3300 to 3200BC discovered in the [Central Eastern Alps](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Central_Eastern_Alps "Central Eastern Alps"), was found with a 99.7% pure copper axhead; high levels of [arsenic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arsenic "Arsenic") in his hair suggest an involvement in copper smelting.[](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Copper#cite_note-CSA-139) Smelting was probably discovered in China before 2800BC, in Central America around 600AD, and in West Africa about the 9th or 10th century AD.[](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Copper#cite_note-140) [Carbon dating](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carbon_dating "Carbon dating") has established copper mining at [Alderley Edge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alderley_Edge_Mines "Alderley Edge Mines") in [Cheshire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cheshire "Cheshire"), UK between 2280 and 1890BC.[](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Copper#cite_note-141) Experience with copper assisted the development of other metals; specifically copper smelting likely led to the discovery of [iron smelting](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bloomery "Bloomery").[](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Copper#cite_note-CSA-139)
Replica of [Ötzi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/%C3%96tzi "Ötzi")'s copper axe from the [Chalcolithic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chalcolithic "Chalcolithic") Era
Production of non-smelted native copper in the [Old Copper Complex](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Old_Copper_Complex "Old Copper Complex") in Michigan and Wisconsin is dated between 6500 and 3000BC.[](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Copper#cite_note-Pompeani-2021-142)[](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Copper#cite_note-occ-143)[](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Copper#cite_note-144) A copper spearpoint found in Wisconsin has been dated to 6500BC.[](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Copper#cite_note-Pompeani-2021-142) Native Copper usage by the indigenous peoples of the Old Copper Complex from the [Great Lakes region](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_Lakes_region "Great Lakes region") of North America has been radiometrically dated to as far back as 7500BC.[](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Copper#cite_note-Pompeani-2021-142)[](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Copper#cite_note-Bebber-2022-145)[](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Copper#cite_note-146) Indigenous peoples of North America around the [Great Lakes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_Lakes "Great Lakes") may have found 99% pure native copper veins during this time, making it one of the oldest known examples of [copper extraction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Copper_extraction "Copper extraction") in the world.[](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Copper#cite_note-:2-147)[](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Copper#cite_note-Pompeani-2013-148) There is evidence from prehistoric lead pollution from lakes in Michigan that people in the region began mining copper c.6000 BC.[](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Copper#cite_note-Pompeani-2013-148)[](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Copper#cite_note-Pompeani-2021-142) Later evidence suggests that utilitarian copper objects fell increasingly out of use in the Old Copper Complex of North America and a shift towards an increased production of ornamental copper objects occurred.[](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Copper#cite_note-149)
#### Bronze Age
Main article: [Bronze Age](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bronze_Age "Bronze Age")
Copper was used in blue pigments like this "[Egyptian blue](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Egyptian_blue "Egyptian blue")" [faience](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Egyptian_faience "Egyptian faience") saucer and stand from the Bronze Age, [New Kingdom of Egypt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_Kingdom_of_Egypt "New Kingdom of Egypt") (1400–1325BC).
Natural bronze, a type of copper made from ores rich in silicon, arsenic, and (rarely) tin, came into general use in the Balkans around 5500BC.[](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Copper#cite_note-150) Alloying copper with tin to make bronze was first practiced about 4000 years after the discovery of copper smelting, and about 2000 years after "natural bronze" had come into general use.[](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Copper#cite_note-151) Bronze artifacts from the [Vinča culture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vin%C4%8Da_culture "Vinča culture") date to 4500BC.[](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Copper#cite_note-antiquity1312-152) [Sumerian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sumer "Sumer") and [Egyptian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_Egypt "Ancient Egypt") artifacts of copper and bronze alloys date to 3000BC.[](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Copper#cite_note-hist-153) [Egyptian blue](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Egyptian_blue "Egyptian blue"), or cuprorivaite (calcium copper silicate), is a synthetic pigment that contains copper and started being used in ancient Egypt around 3250BC.[](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Copper#cite_note-154) The manufacturing process of Egyptian blue was known to the Romans, but by the fourth century AD the pigment fell out of use and the secret to its manufacturing process became lost. The Roman [Vitruvius](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vitruvius "Vitruvius") said in the first century BC that the blue pigment was made from copper minerals or bronze, lime, and a [flux](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flux_\(metallurgy\) "Flux \(metallurgy\)") like [natron](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Natron "Natron"), and this basic recipe has been confirmed in modern times.[](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Copper#cite_note-155)
The [Bronze Age](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bronze_Age "Bronze Age") began in Southeastern Europe around 3700–3300BC, in Northwestern Europe about 2500BC. It ended with the beginning of the Iron Age, 2000–1000BC in the Near East, and 600BC in Northern Europe. The transition between the [Neolithic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neolithic "Neolithic") period and the Bronze Age was formerly termed the [Chalcolithic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chalcolithic "Chalcolithic") period (copper-stone), when copper tools were used with stone tools. The term has gradually fallen out of favor because in some parts of the world, the Chalcolithic and Neolithic are coterminous at both ends. Brass, an alloy of copper and zinc, is of much more recent origin. It was known to the Greeks, but became a significant supplement to bronze during the Roman Empire.[](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Copper#cite_note-hist-153)
### Ancient and post-classical
In [alchemy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alchemy "Alchemy") the symbol for copper was also the symbol for the [goddess](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Venus_\(mythology\) "Venus \(mythology\)") and planet [Venus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Venus "Venus"). Chalcolithic copper mine in [Timna Valley](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Timna_Valley "Timna Valley"), [Negev Desert](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Negev_Desert "Negev Desert"), Israel
In Greece, copper was known by the name _chalkos_ (χαλκός _aes Cyprium_ _aes_ _Cyprium_[Cyprus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cyprus "Cyprus"), where much copper was mined. The phrase was simplified to _cuprum_ _copper_. [Aphrodite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aphrodite "Aphrodite") ([Venus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Venus_\(goddess\) "Venus \(goddess\)") in Rome) represented copper in mythology and alchemy because of its lustrous beauty and its ancient use in producing mirrors; Cyprus, the source of copper, was sacred to the goddess. The seven heavenly bodies known to the ancients were associated with the seven metals known in antiquity, and Venus was assigned to copper, both because of the connection to the goddess and because Venus was the brightest heavenly body after the Sun and Moon and so corresponded to the most lustrous and desirable metal after gold and silver.[](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Copper#cite_note-156)
Copper was the most extensively used metal among natives of North America, with evidence for use going back 7000 years.[](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Copper#cite_note-157) Native copper is known to have been extracted from sites on [Isle Royale](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isle_Royale "Isle Royale") with primitive stone tools between 800 and 1600AD.[](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Copper#cite_note-158) Copper, probably from pure nuggets found in the [Great Lakes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_Lakes "Great Lakes") area, was worked by repeated hammering and annealing in the North American city of [Cahokia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cahokia "Cahokia") (near modern-day Missouri) around 1000–1300AD.[](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Copper#cite_note-Chastain-2011-159) There are several exquisite copper plates, known as the [Mississippian copper plates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mississippian_copper_plates "Mississippian copper plates"), that have been found in North America in the area around Cahokia, dating from this time period (1000–1300AD).[](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Copper#cite_note-Chastain-2011-159)
[Mississippian copper plates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mississippian_copper_plates "Mississippian copper plates") from North America were produced in this style from around 800 to 1600AD.
In South America, a copper mask dated to 1000BC found in the Argentinian Andes is the oldest known copper artifact discovered in the Andes.[](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Copper#cite_note-Cort%C3%A9s-2017-160) Peru has been considered the origin for early copper [metallurgy in pre-Columbian America](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metallurgy_in_pre-Columbian_America "Metallurgy in pre-Columbian America"), but the copper mask from Argentina suggests that the [Cajón del Maipo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Caj%C3%B3n_del_Maipo "Cajón del Maipo") of the southern Andes was another important center for early copper workings in South America.[](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Copper#cite_note-Cort%C3%A9s-2017-160) Copper metallurgy in Peru dates to around 500BC, with larger scale production beginning around 900AD as part of the rise of the [Sican culture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sican_culture "Sican culture") in northern Peru. The production continued through a series of conquests by the [Chimor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chimor "Chimor") and [Inca](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inca "Inca") cultures, ending with the Spanish conquest in 1532.[](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Copper#cite_note-161)
In Sub-Saharan Africa, throughout the period from the first mines around 2000 BC in [Agades](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Agades "Agades") until the early 1800s, copper was viewed as more precious and prestigious than either gold or silver. Copper was widely traded in Africa and fulfilled a number of religious, political, and social functions. By contrast, local deposit of gold were ignored until contact with Portuguese and Arab traders.[](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Copper#cite_note-162)[](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Copper#cite_note-163)[](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Copper#cite_note-164)
The cultural role of copper has been important, particularly in currency. Romans in the 6th through 3rd centuries BC used copper lumps as money. At first, the copper itself was valued, but gradually the shape and look of the copper became more important. [Julius Caesar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Julius_Caesar "Julius Caesar") had his own coins made from brass, while [Octavianus Augustus Caesar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Augustus "Augustus")'s coins were made from Cu-Pb-Sn alloys. With an estimated annual output of around 15,000t, [Roman copper mining and smelting activities](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roman_metallurgy "Roman metallurgy") reached a scale unsurpassed until the time of the [Industrial Revolution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Industrial_Revolution "Industrial Revolution"); the [provinces](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roman_province "Roman province") most intensely mined were those of [Hispania](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hispania "Hispania"), Cyprus and in Central Europe.[](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Copper#cite_note-165)[](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Copper#cite_note-166)
The gates of the [Temple of Jerusalem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Temple_of_Jerusalem "Temple of Jerusalem") used [Corinthian bronze](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Corinthian_bronze "Corinthian bronze"), a copper, silver, and gold alloy treated with [depletion gilding](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Depletion_gilding "Depletion gilding"), which successively removes oxidized copper to create a gold surface coat. The process was most prevalent in [Alexandria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alexandria "Alexandria"), where alchemy, inspired by the chemical treatment resulting in gold appearance, is thought to have begun.[](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Copper#cite_note-167)
### Modern
18th-century copper [kettle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kettle "Kettle") from Norway made from Swedish copper
The [Great Copper Mountain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_Copper_Mountain "Great Copper Mountain") was a mine in [Falun](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Falun "Falun"), Sweden, that operated from the 10th century to 1992. It satisfied two-thirds of Europe's copper consumption in the 17th century and helped fund many of Sweden's wars during that time.[](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Copper#cite_note-168) It was referred to as the nation's treasury; Sweden had a [copper backed currency](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_copper_currency_in_Sweden "History of copper currency in Sweden").[](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Copper#cite_note-169)
[Chalcography](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chalcography "Chalcography") of the city of [Vyborg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vyborg "Vyborg") at the turn of the 17th and 18th centuries. The year 1709 carved on the printing plate.
Copper is used in roofing, currency, and for photographic technology known as the [daguerreotype](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Daguerreotype "Daguerreotype"). Copper was used in [Renaissance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Renaissance "Renaissance") sculpture, and was used to construct the [Statue of Liberty](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Statue_of_Liberty "Statue of Liberty"); copper continues to be used in construction of various types. [Copper plating](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Copper_electroplating "Copper electroplating") and [copper sheathing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Copper_sheathing "Copper sheathing") were widely used to protect the under-water hulls of ships, a technique pioneered by the British Admiralty in the 18th century.[](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Copper#cite_note-170) The [Norddeutsche Affinerie](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Norddeutsche_Affinerie "Norddeutsche Affinerie") in Hamburg was the first modern [electroplating](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electroplating "Electroplating") plant, starting its production in 1876.[](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Copper#cite_note-171)
During the rise in demand for copper for the Age of Electricity, from the 1880s until the Great Depression of the 1930s, the United States produced one third to half the world's newly mined copper.[](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Copper#cite_note-172) Major districts included the Keweenaw district of northern Michigan, primarily native copper deposits, which was eclipsed by the vast sulphide deposits of [Butte, Montana](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Butte,_Montana "Butte, Montana"), in the late 1880s, which itself was eclipsed by porphyry deposits of the Southwest United States, especially at [Bingham Canyon, Utah](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bingham_Canyon,_Utah "Bingham Canyon, Utah"), and [Morenci, Arizona](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Morenci,_Arizona "Morenci, Arizona"). Introduction of open pit steam shovel mining and innovations in smelting, refining, flotation concentration and other processing steps led to mass production. Early in the twentieth century, [Arizona](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arizona "Arizona") ranked first, followed by [Montana](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Montana "Montana"), then [Utah](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Utah "Utah") and [Michigan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Michigan "Michigan").[](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Copper#cite_note-173)
[Flash smelting](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flash_smelting "Flash smelting") was developed by [Outokumpu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Outokumpu "Outokumpu") in Finland and first applied at [Harjavalta](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harjavalta "Harjavalta") in 1949; the energy-efficient process accounts for 50% of the world's primary copper production.[](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Copper#cite_note-174)
The [Intergovernmental Council of Copper Exporting Countries](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Intergovernmental_Council_of_Copper_Exporting_Countries "Intergovernmental Council of Copper Exporting Countries"), formed in 1967 by Chile, Peru, Zaire, and Zambia, operated in the copper market as [OPEC](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/OPEC "OPEC") does in oil, though it never achieved the same influence, particularly because the second-largest producer, the United States, was never a member; it was dissolved in 1988.[](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Copper#cite_note-175) In 2008, China became the world's largest importer of copper.:187
## Applications
See also: [Copper in renewable energy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Copper_in_renewable_energy "Copper in renewable energy")
Copper fittings for soldered plumbing joints
The major applications of copper are electrical wire (60%), roofing and plumbing (20%), and industrial machinery (15%). Copper is used mostly as a pure metal, but when greater hardness is required, it is put into such alloys as [brass](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brass "Brass") and [bronze](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bronze "Bronze") (5% of total use). For more than two centuries, copper paint has been used on boat hulls to control the growth of plants and shellfish.[](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Copper#cite_note-176) A small part of the copper supply is used for nutritional supplements and fungicides in agriculture.[](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Copper#cite_note-Applications_for_Copper-177) Pure copper's ductility, weakness and high friction between copper chips and cutting tools makes [machining](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Machining "Machining") of copper difficult; alloys are preferred for good [machinability](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Machinability "Machinability").[](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Copper#cite_note-178) Copper [cookware](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cookware_and_bakeware "Cookware and bakeware") can react with acidic or alkaline foods, leaving a metallic taste, but is useful for whipping egg-whites.[](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Copper#cite_note-179)
### Wire and cable
Main article: [Copper wire and cable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Copper_wire_and_cable "Copper wire and cable")
Despite competition from other materials, copper remains the preferred [electrical conductor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electrical_conductor "Electrical conductor") in nearly all categories of electrical wiring except overhead [electric power transmission](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electric_power_transmission "Electric power transmission") where [aluminium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aluminium "Aluminium") is often preferred.[](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Copper#cite_note-180)[](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Copper#cite_note-181) Copper wire is used in [power generation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Power_generation "Power generation"), [power transmission](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Power_transmission "Power transmission"), [electric power distribution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electric_power_distribution "Electric power distribution"), [telecommunications](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Telecommunications "Telecommunications"), [electronics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electronics "Electronics") circuitry, and countless types of [electrical equipment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electrical_equipment "Electrical equipment").[](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Copper#cite_note-182) [Electrical wiring](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electrical_wiring "Electrical wiring") is the most important market for the copper industry.[](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Copper#cite_note-183) This includes structural power wiring, power distribution cable, appliance wire, communications cable, automotive wire and cable, and magnet wire. Roughly half of all copper mined is used for electrical wire and cable conductors.[](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Copper#cite_note-184) Many electrical devices rely on copper wiring because of its multitude of beneficial properties, such as its high [electrical conductivity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electrical_conductivity "Electrical conductivity"), [tensile strength](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tensile_strength "Tensile strength"), [ductility](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ductility "Ductility"), [creep (deformation)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Creep_\(deformation\) "Creep \(deformation\)") resistance, [corrosion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Corrosion "Corrosion") resistance, low [thermal expansion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thermal_expansion "Thermal expansion"), high [thermal conductivity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thermal_conductivity "Thermal conductivity"), ease of [soldering](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solder "Solder"), and ease of installation.[](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Copper#cite_note-185):5.3
For a short period from the late 1960s to the late 1970s, copper wiring was replaced by [aluminium wiring](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aluminium_wiring "Aluminium wiring") in many housing construction projects in America but improper design resulted in fire hazards.[](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Copper#cite_note-186)[](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Copper#cite_note-187) The safety issues have since been solved by use of larger sizes of aluminium wire (#8AWG and up), and properly designed aluminium wiring is still being installed in place of copper. For example, the [Airbus A380](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Airbus_A380 "Airbus A380") uses aluminum wire in place of copper wire for electrical power transmission.[](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Copper#cite_note-188)
### Electronics and related devices
Copper electrical [busbars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Busbar "Busbar") distributing power to a large building
[Integrated circuits](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Integrated_circuit "Integrated circuit") and [printed circuit boards](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Printed_circuit_board "Printed circuit board") increasingly feature copper in place of aluminium because of its superior electrical conductivity; [heat sinks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heat_sink "Heat sink") and [heat exchangers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heat_exchanger "Heat exchanger") use copper because of its superior heat dissipation properties. [Electromagnets](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electromagnet "Electromagnet"), [vacuum tubes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vacuum_tube "Vacuum tube"), [cathode-ray tubes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cathode-ray_tube "Cathode-ray tube"), and [magnetrons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magnetron "Magnetron") in microwave ovens use copper, as do [waveguides](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Waveguide "Waveguide") for microwave radiation.[](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Copper#cite_note-189)
### Electric motors
Copper's superior [conductivity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Copper_wire_and_cable#Electrical_conductivity "Copper wire and cable") enhances the efficiency of electrical [motors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Motor_\(device\) "Motor \(device\)").[](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Copper#cite_note-190) This is important because motors and motor-driven systems account for 43–46% of all global electricity consumption and 69% of all electricity used by industry.[](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Copper#cite_note-191) Increasing the mass and cross section of copper in a [coil](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inductor "Inductor") increases the efficiency of the motor. [Copper motor rotors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Induction_motor "Induction motor"), a new technology designed for motor applications where energy savings are prime design objectives,[](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Copper#cite_note-192)[](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Copper#cite_note-193) are enabling general-purpose induction motors to meet and exceed [National Electrical Manufacturers Association](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Electrical_Manufacturers_Association "National Electrical Manufacturers Association") (NEMA) [premium efficiency](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Premium_efficiency "Premium efficiency") standards.[](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Copper#cite_note-194)

Main article: [Copper in architecture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Copper_in_architecture "Copper in architecture")
Copper roof on the [Minneapolis City Hall](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Minneapolis_City_Hall "Minneapolis City Hall"), coated with [patina](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Patina "Patina")
Copper has been used since ancient times as a durable, [corrosion resistant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Corrosion_resistance "Corrosion resistance"), and weatherproof architectural material. [Roofs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roofing_material "Roofing material"), [flashings](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flashing_\(weatherproofing\) "Flashing \(weatherproofing\)"), [rain gutters](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rain_gutter "Rain gutter"), [downspouts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Downspout "Downspout"), [domes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dome "Dome"), [spires](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spire "Spire"), vaults, and [doors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Door "Door") have been made from copper for hundreds or thousands of years. Copper's architectural use has been expanded in modern times to include interior and exterior [wall cladding](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Copper_in_architecture#Wall_cladding "Copper in architecture"), building [expansion joints](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Expansion_joint "Expansion joint"), [radio frequency shielding](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/RF_shielding "RF shielding"), and [antimicrobial](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antimicrobial_copper-alloy_touch_surfaces "Antimicrobial copper-alloy touch surfaces") and decorative indoor products such as attractive handrails, bathroom fixtures, and counter tops. Some of copper's other important benefits as an architectural material include low [thermal movement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thermal_expansion "Thermal expansion"), light weight, [lightning protection](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lightning_rod "Lightning rod"), and recyclability.[](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Copper#cite_note-195)[](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Copper#cite_note-196)[](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Copper#cite_note-197)[](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Copper#cite_note-198)[]
The metal's distinctive natural green [patina](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Patina "Patina") has long been coveted by architects and designers. The final patina is a particularly durable layer that is highly resistant to atmospheric corrosion, thereby protecting the underlying metal against further weathering.[](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Copper#cite_note-199)[](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Copper#cite_note-200)[](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Copper#cite_note-201) It can be a mixture of carbonate and sulfate compounds in various amounts, depending upon environmental conditions such as sulfur-containing acid rain.[](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Copper#cite_note-202)[](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Copper#cite_note-203)[](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Copper#cite_note-204)[](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Copper#cite_note-205)[] Architectural copper and its alloys can also be ['finished'](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Copper_in_architecture#Finishes "Copper in architecture") to take on a particular look, feel, or color. Finishes include mechanical surface treatments, chemical coloring, and coatings.[](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Copper#cite_note-206)
Copper has excellent [brazing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brazing "Brazing") and [soldering](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soldering "Soldering") properties and can be [welded](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Welded "Welded"); the best results are obtained with [gas metal arc welding](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gas_metal_arc_welding "Gas metal arc welding").[](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Copper#cite_note-207)
### Antibiofouling
Main articles: [Copper alloys in aquaculture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Copper_alloys_in_aquaculture "Copper alloys in aquaculture") and [Copper sheathing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Copper_sheathing "Copper sheathing")
Copper is [biostatic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biostatic "Biostatic"), meaning bacteria and many other forms of life will not grow on it. For this reason it has long been used to line parts of ships to protect against [barnacles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barnacle "Barnacle") and [mussels](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mussel "Mussel"). It was originally used pure, but has since been superseded by [Muntz metal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Muntz_metal "Muntz metal") and copper-based paint. Similarly, as discussed in [copper alloys in aquaculture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Copper_alloys_in_aquaculture "Copper alloys in aquaculture"), copper alloys have become important netting materials in the [aquaculture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aquaculture "Aquaculture") industry because they are [antimicrobial](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antimicrobial "Antimicrobial") and prevent [biofouling](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biofouling "Biofouling"), even in extreme conditions,[](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Copper#cite_note-208) and have strong structural and [corrosion-resistant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Corrosion-resistant "Corrosion-resistant") properties in marine environments.[](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Copper#cite_note-209)
### Antimicrobial
Main articles: [Antimicrobial properties of copper](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antimicrobial_properties_of_copper "Antimicrobial properties of copper") and [Antimicrobial copper-alloy touch surfaces](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antimicrobial_copper-alloy_touch_surfaces "Antimicrobial copper-alloy touch surfaces")
[Copper-alloy touch surfaces](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antimicrobial_copper-alloy_touch_surfaces "Antimicrobial copper-alloy touch surfaces") have natural properties that destroy a wide range of [microorganisms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Microorganisms "Microorganisms") (e.g., O157:H7, [methicillin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Methicillin "Methicillin")-resistant ([MRSA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Methicillin-resistant_Staphylococcus_aureus "Methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus")), , , [influenza A virus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Influenza_A_virus "Influenza A virus"), [adenovirus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adenoviridae "Adenoviridae"), [SARS-CoV-2](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SARS-CoV-2 "SARS-CoV-2"), and [fungi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fungus "Fungus")).[](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Copper#cite_note-Copper_Touch_Surfaces-210)[](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Copper#cite_note-211) Some copper alloys were proven to kill more than 99.9% of disease-causing bacteria within just two hours when cleaned regularly.[](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Copper#cite_note-US_EPA-2008-212) The [United States Environmental Protection Agency](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_Environmental_Protection_Agency "United States Environmental Protection Agency") (EPA) has approved the registrations of these copper alloys as "[antimicrobial](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antimicrobial "Antimicrobial") materials with public health benefits";[](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Copper#cite_note-US_EPA-2008-212) that approval allows manufacturers to make legal claims to the public health benefits of products made of registered alloys. In addition, the EPA has approved a long list of antimicrobial copper products made from these alloys, such as bedrails, [handrails](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Handrails "Handrails"), over-bed tables, [sinks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sinks "Sinks"), [faucets](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Faucets "Faucets"), [door knobs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Door_knobs "Door knobs"), [toilet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Toilet "Toilet") hardware, [computer keyboards](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Computer_keyboards "Computer keyboards"), [health club](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Health_club "Health club") equipment, and [shopping cart](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shopping_cart "Shopping cart") handles. Copper doorknobs are used by hospitals to minimize the transfer of disease, and [Legionnaires' disease](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Legionnaires'_disease "Legionnaires' disease") is suppressed by copper tubing in plumbing systems.[](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Copper#cite_note-213) Antimicrobial copper alloy products are now being installed in healthcare facilities in the UK, Ireland, Japan, Korea, France, Denmark, and Brazil, and in the subway transit system in Santiago, Chile, where copper–zinc alloy handrails were installed in some 30 stations between 2011 and 2014.[](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Copper#cite_note-214)[](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Copper#cite_note-215)[](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Copper#cite_note-216) Copper surfaces in healthcare have been advocated for in the US, as well.[](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Copper#cite_note-217) Textile fibers can be blended with copper to create antimicrobial protective fabrics.[](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Copper#cite_note-218)
### Fungicide
Copper is widely used as a wood preservative primarily because it is an effective [fungicide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fungicide "Fungicide") against [soft rot fungi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wood-decay_fungus#Soft_rot "Wood-decay fungus") while avoiding the significant environmental impact of [chromium and arsenic based preservatives](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chromated_copper_arsenate "Chromated copper arsenate").[](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Copper#cite_note-219)
### Folk medicine
Copper is commonly used in jewelry, and according to some folklore, copper bracelets relieve [arthritis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arthritis "Arthritis") symptoms.[](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Copper#cite_note-220) In one trial for osteoarthritis and one trial for rheumatoid arthritis, no differences were found between copper bracelet and control (non-copper) bracelet.[](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Copper#cite_note-221)[](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Copper#cite_note-RichmondBrown2009-222) No evidence shows that copper can be absorbed through the skin.[](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Copper#cite_note-223)
