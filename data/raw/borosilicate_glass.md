From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
High-strength glass, made of silica and boron trioxide
[![A PYREX 1-quart measuring cup, featuring graduations in United States customary units](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/4/47/Pyrex_1-quart_liquid_measuring_cup_-_DPLA_-_b07e5efc0a37cf693d74b366a3065c72.jpg/330px-Pyrex_1-quart_liquid_measuring_cup_-_DPLA_-_b07e5efc0a37cf693d74b366a3065c72.jpg?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Pyrex_1-quart_liquid_measuring_cup_-_DPLA_-_b07e5efc0a37cf693d74b366a3065c72.jpg)[Pyrex](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pyrex "Pyrex") is a type of borosilicate glass developed by [Corning](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Corning_Inc. "Corning Inc.") in 1908, and introduced to the public in 1915 as a line of kitchenware. Pyrex kitchen products no longer use borosilicate in all regions.
**Borosilicate glass** is a type of [glass](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glass "Glass") with [silica](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Silicon_dioxide "Silicon dioxide") and [boron trioxide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boron_trioxide "Boron trioxide") as the main glass-forming constituents. The glass has numerous advantages over regular [soda–lime glass](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soda%E2%80%93lime_glass "Soda–lime glass"), including being harder to break when dropped; more resistant to cracking from significant changes in temperature; and having a higher clarity (transparency), which is maintained over a longer period of time. Borosilicate is generally slightly more expensive than soda-lime glass.[[1]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Borosilicate_glass#cite_note-Borosilicate_Glass_vs._Regular_Glass:_Which_One_is_Better?-1)
Borosilicate glasses are known for having very low [coefficients of thermal expansion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coefficient_of_thermal_expansion "Coefficient of thermal expansion") (≈3×10−6 K−1 at 20°C), making them more resistant to [thermal shock](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thermal_shock "Thermal shock") than any other common glass. Such glass is subjected to less [thermal stress](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thermal_stress "Thermal stress") and can withstand temperature differentials of about 330°F (166°C) without [fracturing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thermal_fracturing_in_glass "Thermal fracturing in glass").[[2]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Borosilicate_glass#cite_note-diff-2) It is commonly used for the construction of [reagent bottles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reagent_bottle "Reagent bottle") and [flasks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Laboratory_flask "Laboratory flask"), as well as lighting, electronics, and cookware. For many other applications, soda–lime glass is more common.[_[citation needed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed "Wikipedia:Citation needed")_]
Borosilicate glass is sold under various trade names, including [Borosil](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Borosil "Borosil"), [Duran](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Duran_\(glass\) "Duran \(glass\)"), [Pyrex](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pyrex "Pyrex"), Glassco, Supertek, Suprax, Simax, Bellco, Marinex (Brazil), [BSA 60](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BSA_60?action=edit&redlink=1 "BSA 60 \(page does not exist\)"), [BSC 51](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BSC_51?action=edit&redlink=1 "BSC 51 \(page does not exist\)") (by [NIPRO](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/NIPRO?action=edit&redlink=1 "NIPRO \(page does not exist\)")), Heatex, Endural, [Schott](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Schott_AG "Schott AG"), Refmex, Kimax, Gemstone Well, United Scientific, and MG (India).[_[citation needed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed "Wikipedia:Citation needed")_]
Single-ended self-starting lamps are insulated with a [mica](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mica "Mica") disc and contained in a borosilicate glass gas discharge tube (arc tube) and a metal cap.[[3]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Borosilicate_glass#cite_note-auto1-3)[[4]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Borosilicate_glass#cite_note-auto-4) They include the [sodium-vapor lamp](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sodium-vapor_lamp "Sodium-vapor lamp") that is commonly used in street lighting.[[5]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Borosilicate_glass#cite_note-5)[[6]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Borosilicate_glass#cite_note-6)[[3]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Borosilicate_glass#cite_note-auto1-3)[[4]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Borosilicate_glass#cite_note-auto-4)
Borosilicate glass usually melts at about 1,650°C (3,000°F; 1,920K).[_[citation needed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed "Wikipedia:Citation needed")_]
## History
[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Borosilicate_glass&action=edit&section=1 "Edit section: History")]
[![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/3/35/Bromine_vial_in_acrylic_cube.jpg/330px-Bromine_vial_in_acrylic_cube.jpg?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Bromine_vial_in_acrylic_cube.jpg)[Bromine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bromine "Bromine") sealed inside a borosilicate glass vial, which itself is sealed inside a ~5cm cube of [PMMA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Poly\(methyl_methacrylate\) "Poly\(methyl methacrylate\)") (acrylic)
Borosilicate glass was first developed by German glassmaker [Otto Schott](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Otto_Schott "Otto Schott") in the late 19th century in [Jena](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jena "Jena"). This early borosilicate glass thus came to be known as [Jena glass](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jena_glass "Jena glass").[_[citation needed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed "Wikipedia:Citation needed")_]
After [Corning Glass Works](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Corning_Incorporated "Corning Incorporated") introduced [Pyrex](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pyrex "Pyrex") in 1915, the name became synonymous with borosilicate glass in the English-speaking world (although since the 1940s, a sizeable portion of glass produced under the Pyrex brand has also been made of [soda–lime glass](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soda%E2%80%93lime_glass "Soda–lime glass")). 
Borosilicate glass is the name of a glass family with various members tailored to completely different purposes. Most common today is borosilicate 3.3 or 5.0x glass such as Duran, Corning33, Corning51-V (clear), Corning51-L (amber), International Cookware's NIPRO BSA 60, and BSC 51.[_[citation needed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed "Wikipedia:Citation needed")_]
## Manufacturing process
[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Borosilicate_glass&action=edit&section=2 "Edit section: Manufacturing process")]
Borosilicate glass is created by combining and melting [boric oxide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boron_trioxide "Boron trioxide"), silica sand, [soda ash](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sodium_carbonate "Sodium carbonate"),[[7]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Borosilicate_glass#cite_note-7) and alumina. Since borosilicate glass melts at a higher temperature than ordinary [silicate glass](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Silicate_glass "Silicate glass"), some new techniques were required for industrial production.[_[citation needed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed "Wikipedia:Citation needed")_]
In addition to [quartz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quartz "Quartz"), [sodium carbonate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sodium_carbonate "Sodium carbonate"), and [aluminium oxide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aluminium_oxide "Aluminium oxide") traditionally used in [glassmaking](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glass_production "Glass production"), [boron](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boron "Boron") is used in the manufacture of borosilicate glass. The composition of low-expansion borosilicate glass, such as those laboratory glasses mentioned above, is approximately 80% [silica](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Silicon_dioxide "Silicon dioxide"), 13% [boric oxide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boric_oxide "Boric oxide"), 4% [sodium oxide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sodium_oxide "Sodium oxide") or [potassium oxide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Potassium_oxide "Potassium oxide") and 2–3% [aluminium oxide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aluminium_oxide "Aluminium oxide"). Though more difficult to make than traditional glass due to its high melting temperature, it is economical to produce. Its superior durability, chemical and heat resistance finds use in [chemical](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chemistry "Chemistry") [laboratory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Laboratory "Laboratory") equipment, cookware, lighting, and in certain kinds of windows.[_[citation needed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed "Wikipedia:Citation needed")_]
The manufacturing process depends on the product geometry and can be differentiated between different methods like [floating](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Float_glass "Float glass"), [tube drawing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glass_tube "Glass tube"), or [molding](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Molding_\(process\) "Molding \(process\)").
## Physical characteristics
[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Borosilicate_glass&action=edit&section=3 "Edit section: Physical characteristics")]
The common type of borosilicate glass used for laboratory glassware has a very low [thermal expansion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thermal_expansion "Thermal expansion") coefficient (3.3 × 10−6 K−1),[[8]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Borosilicate_glass#cite_note-8) about one-third that of ordinary soda–lime glass. This reduces material stresses caused by temperature gradients, which makes borosilicate a more suitable type of glass for certain applications.
[Fused quartzware](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fused_quartz "Fused quartz") is even better in this respect (having one-fifteenth the thermal expansion of soda–lime glass); however, the difficulty of working with fused quartz makes quartzware much more expensive, and borosilicate glass is a low-cost compromise. While more resistant to [thermal shock](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thermal_shock "Thermal shock") than other types of glass, borosilicate glass can still crack or shatter when subjected to rapid or uneven temperature variations.
Among the characteristic properties of this glass family are:
  * Different borosilicate glasses cover a wide range of different thermal expansions, enabling direct seals with various metals and alloys like [molybdenum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Molybdenum "Molybdenum") glass with a CTE ([coefficient of thermal expansion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coefficient_of_thermal_expansion "Coefficient of thermal expansion")) of 4.6, [tungsten](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tungsten "Tungsten") with a CTE around 4.0 and [Kovar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kovar "Kovar") with a CTE around 5.0 because of the matched CTE with the sealing partner
  * Allowing high maximum temperatures of typically about 500°C (930°F)[[9]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Borosilicate_glass#cite_note-9)
  * Showing an extremely high chemical resistance in corrosive environments. Norm tests for example for [acid resistance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Corrosion "Corrosion") create extreme conditions and reveal very low impacts on glass


The [softening point](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Softening_point "Softening point") (temperature at which [viscosity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Viscosity "Viscosity") is approximately 107.6 [poise](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Poise_\(unit\) "Poise \(unit\)")) of type 7740 Pyrex is 820°C (1,510°F).[[10]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Borosilicate_glass#cite_note-10)
Borosilicate glass is less [dense](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Density "Density") (about 2.23 g/cm3) than typical soda–lime glass due to the low atomic mass of boron. Its mean specific heat capacity at constant pressure (20–100°C) is 0.83 J/(g⋅K), roughly one fifth of water's.[[11]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Borosilicate_glass#cite_note-11)
The temperature differential that borosilicate glass can withstand before fracturing is about 330°F (170°C), whereas soda–lime glass can withstand only about a 100°F (40°C) change in temperature. This is why typical kitchenware made from traditional soda–lime glass (including some modern Pyrex kitchenware[[12]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Borosilicate_glass#cite_note-12)) will shatter if a vessel containing boiling water is placed on ice, but borosilicate-glass Pyrex or other borosilicate laboratory glass will not.[[2]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Borosilicate_glass#cite_note-diff-2)
Optically, borosilicate glasses are [crown glasses](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crown_glass_\(optics\) "Crown glass \(optics\)") with low dispersion ([Abbe numbers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abbe_number "Abbe number") around 65) and relatively low [refractive indices](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Refractive_index "Refractive index") (1.51–1.54 across the visible range).
During manufacture, molten borosilicates tend to have higher liquid 'fragilities' than silicates. This refers to the fact that the viscosity of the molten glass varies more rapidly than exponentially with temperature, and is said to be super-Arrhenian, with the fragility index increasing with boron content. This behaviour can be partly explained by structural changes, including reversible depolymerisation of the glass network upon heating, concomitant with changes in the boron bonding environment, from tetrahedral four-coordinated at lower temperatures, toward trigonal planar three-coordinated at higher temperatures.[[13]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Borosilicate_glass#cite_note-13)
### Families
[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Borosilicate_glass&action=edit&section=4 "Edit section: Families")]
For the purposes of classification, borosilicate glass can be roughly arranged in the following groups, according to their oxide composition (in [mass fractions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mass_fraction_\(chemistry\) "Mass fraction \(chemistry\)")). Characteristic of borosilicate glasses is the presence of substantial amounts of silica (SiO2) and boric oxide (B2O3, >8%) as glass network formers.
The amount of boric oxide affects the glass properties in a particular way. Apart from the highly resistant varieties (B2O3 up to a maximum of 13%), there are others that – due to the different way in which the boric oxide is incorporated into the structural network – have only low chemical resistance (B2O3 content over 15%).[[14]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Borosilicate_glass#cite_note-Schott-cat-14) Hence we differentiate between the following subtypes.
#### Non-alkaline-earth
[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Borosilicate_glass&action=edit&section=5 "Edit section: Non-alkaline-earth")]
The B2O3 content for borosilicate glass is typically 12–13% and the SiO2 content over 80%. High chemical durability and low thermal expansion (3.3 × 10−6 K−1) – the lowest of all commercial glasses for large-scale technical applications – make this a versatile glass material.
High-grade borosilicate flat glasses are used in a wide variety of industries, mainly for technical applications that require either good thermal resistance, excellent chemical durability, or high light transmission in combination with a pristine surface quality. Other typical applications for different forms of borosilicate glass include glass tubing, glass [piping](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Piping "Piping"), glass containers, etc. especially for the chemical industry.
#### Alkaline-earth
[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Borosilicate_glass&action=edit&section=6 "Edit section: Alkaline-earth")]
In addition to about 75% SiO2 and 8–12% B2O3, these glasses contain up to 5% oxides of [alkaline earth metal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alkaline_earths "Alkaline earths") and alumina (Al2O3). This is a subtype of slightly softer glasses, which have thermal expansions in the range (4.0–5.0) × 10−6 K−1.[[15]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Borosilicate_glass#cite_note-Pires-2009-15)
This is not to be confused with simple borosilicate glass-alumina composites.[[16]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Borosilicate_glass#cite_note-Lima-2012-16)
#### High-borate
[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Borosilicate_glass&action=edit&section=7 "Edit section: High-borate")]
Glasses containing 15–25% B2O3, 65–70% SiO2, and smaller amounts of alkalis and Al2O3 as additional components have low softening points and low thermal expansion. Sealability to metals in the expansion range of tungsten and molybdenum and high electrical insulation are their most important features.
The increased B2O3 content reduces the chemical resistance; in this respect, high-borate borosilicate glasses differ widely from non-alkaline-earth and alkaline-earth borosilicate glasses. Among these are also borosilicate glasses that transmit UV light down to 180nm, which combine the best of the borosilicate glass and the quartz world.[[14]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Borosilicate_glass#cite_note-Schott-cat-14)
## Uses
[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Borosilicate_glass&action=edit&section=8 "Edit section: Uses")]
Borosilicate glass has a wide variety of uses ranging from cookware to lab equipment, as well as a component of high-quality products such as implantable [medical devices](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Medical_devices "Medical devices") and devices used in [space exploration](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Space_exploration "Space exploration").[_[citation needed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed "Wikipedia:Citation needed")_]
### Health and science
[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Borosilicate_glass&action=edit&section=9 "Edit section: Health and science")]
[![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/1/12/Beakers.jpg/250px-Beakers.jpg?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Beakers.jpg)Borosilicate beakers
Virtually all modern laboratory glassware is made of borosilicate glass. It is widely used in this application due to its chemical and thermal resistance and good optical clarity, but the glass can react with [sodium hydride](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sodium_hydride "Sodium hydride") upon heating to produce [sodium borohydride](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sodium_borohydride "Sodium borohydride"), a common laboratory reducing agent.
[Fused quartz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fused_quartz "Fused quartz") is also found in some laboratory equipment when its higher melting point and transmission of UV are required (e.g. for tube furnace liners and UV [cuvettes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cuvette "Cuvette")), but the cost and manufacturing difficulties associated with fused quartz make it an impractical investment for the majority of laboratory equipment.[_[citation needed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed "Wikipedia:Citation needed")_]
Additionally, borosilicate tubing is used as the feedstock for the production of [parenteral](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parenteral "Parenteral") drug packaging, such as vials and pre-filled [syringes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Syringe "Syringe"), as well as [ampoules](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ampoule "Ampoule") and [dental cartridges](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dental_syringe "Dental syringe"). The chemical resistance of borosilicate glass minimizes the migration of sodium ions from the glass matrix, thus making it well suited for [injectable-drug](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Drug_injection "Drug injection") applications. This type of glass is typically referred to as USP / EP JP Type I.[_[citation needed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed "Wikipedia:Citation needed")_]
Borosilicate is widely used in implantable [medical devices](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Medical_device "Medical device") such as prosthetic eyes, artificial hip joints, bone cements, dental composite materials (white fillings).[[17]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Borosilicate_glass#cite_note-17)
Many implantable devices benefit from the unique advantages of borosilicate glass encapsulation. Applications include [veterinary tracking devices](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Microchip_implant_\(animal\) "Microchip implant \(animal\)"), neurostimulators for the treatment of epilepsy, implantable drug pumps, [cochlear implants](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cochlear_implant "Cochlear implant"), and physiological sensors.[[18]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Borosilicate_glass#cite_note-18)
### Electronics
[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Borosilicate_glass&action=edit&section=10 "Edit section: Electronics")]
During the mid-20th century, borosilicate glass tubing was used to pipe coolants (often [distilled water](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Distilled_water "Distilled water")) through high-power [vacuum-tube](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vacuum-tube "Vacuum-tube")–based electronic equipment, such as commercial broadcast transmitters. It was also used for the envelope material for glass transmitting tubes which operated at high temperatures.
Borosilicate glasses also have an application in the [semiconductor industry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Semiconductor_industry "Semiconductor industry") in the development of [microelectromechanical systems](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Microelectromechanical_systems "Microelectromechanical systems") (MEMS), as part of stacks of [etched](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Etching_\(microfabrication\) "Etching \(microfabrication\)") silicon [wafers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wafer_\(electronics\) "Wafer \(electronics\)") bonded to the etched borosilicate glass.
In 2026, Microsoft's Project Silica was able to use borosilicate wafers as a long term computer storage medium. Estimates say that a wafer measuring 120 mm by 120 mm by 2 mm could store 2.02 terabytes for over 10,000 years [[19]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Borosilicate_glass#cite_note-19)
### Cookware
[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Borosilicate_glass&action=edit&section=11 "Edit section: Cookware")]
[![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/6/6f/Arcopal.JPG/250px-Arcopal.JPG?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Arcopal.JPG)[Arc Holdings](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arc_Holdings "Arc Holdings") bakeware
Cookware is another common usage for borosilicate glass, including bakeware. It is used for some measuring cups, featuring screen printed markings providing graduated measurements. Borosilicate glass is sometimes used for high-quality beverage glassware, particularly in pieces designed for hot drinks. Items made of borosilicate glass can be thin yet durable, or thicker for extra strength, and are [microwave](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Microwave "Microwave")- and dishwasher-safe.[[20]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Borosilicate_glass#cite_note-20)
### Lighting
[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Borosilicate_glass&action=edit&section=12 "Edit section: Lighting")]
Many high-quality flashlights use borosilicate glass for the lens. This increases [light transmittance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Light_transmittance "Light transmittance") through the lens compared to plastics and lower-quality glass.
Several types of [high-intensity discharge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/High-intensity_discharge_lamp "High-intensity discharge lamp") (HID) lamps, such as [mercury-vapor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mercury-vapor_lamp "Mercury-vapor lamp") and [metal-halide lamps](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metal-halide_lamp "Metal-halide lamp"), use borosilicate glass as the outer envelope material.
New [lampworking](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lampworking "Lampworking") techniques led to artistic applications such as contemporary glass [marbles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marble_\(toy\) "Marble \(toy\)"). The modern [studio glass](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Studio_glass "Studio glass") movement has responded to color. Borosilicate is commonly used in the [glassblowing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glassblowing "Glassblowing") form of [lampworking](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lampworking "Lampworking") and the artists create a range of products such as [jewelry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jewelry "Jewelry"), [kitchenware](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kitchenware "Kitchenware"), [sculpture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sculpture "Sculpture"), as well as for artistic glass smoking pipes.
Lighting manufacturers use borosilicate glass in some of their lenses.[_[citation needed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed "Wikipedia:Citation needed")_]
[Organic light-emitting diodes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Organic_light-emitting_diodes "Organic light-emitting diodes") (OLED) (for display and lighting purposes) also use borosilicate glass (BK7). The thicknesses of the BK7 glass substrates are usually less than 1 millimeter for OLED fabrication. Due to its optical and mechanical characteristics in relation with cost, BK7 is a common substrate in OLEDs. However, depending on the application, [soda–lime glass](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soda%E2%80%93lime_glass "Soda–lime glass") substrates of similar thicknesses are also used in OLED fabrication.
### Optics
[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Borosilicate_glass&action=edit&section=13 "Edit section: Optics")]
Many astronomical [reflecting telescopes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reflecting_telescope "Reflecting telescope") use glass mirror components made of borosilicate glass because of its low coefficient of thermal expansion. This makes very precise optical surfaces possible that change very little with temperature, and matched glass mirror components that "track" across temperature changes and retain the optical system's characteristics.
The Hale Telescope's [200 inch mirror](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hale_Telescope#200-inch_mirror "Hale Telescope") is made of borosilicate glass.[[21]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Borosilicate_glass#cite_note-21)
The optical glass most often used for making instrument [lenses](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lens_\(optics\) "Lens \(optics\)") is [Schott](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Schott_AG "Schott AG") BK-7 (or the equivalent from other makers, such as the Chinese crown glass [K9](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/K9_glass "K9 glass")), a very finely made borosilicate [crown glass](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crown_glass_\(optics\) "Crown glass \(optics\)").[[22]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Borosilicate_glass#cite_note-22) It is also designated as 517642 glass after its 1.517 refractive index and 64.2 [Abbe number](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abbe_number "Abbe number"). Other less costly borosilicate glasses, such as Schott B270 or the equivalent, are used to make "[crown-glass](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crown_glass_\(optics\) "Crown glass \(optics\)")" eyeglass lenses.
Ordinary, lower-cost borosilicate glass, like that used to make kitchenware and even reflecting telescope mirrors, cannot be used for high-quality lenses because of the striations and [inclusions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inclusion_\(mineral\) "Inclusion \(mineral\)") common to lower grades of this type of glass. The maximal working temperature is 268°C (514°F). While it transitions to a liquid starting at 288°C (550°F) (just before it turns red-hot), it is not workable until it reaches over 538°C (1,000°F). That means that in order to industrially produce this glass, oxygen/fuel torches must be used. Glassblowers borrowed technology and techniques from welders.
### Rapid prototyping
[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Borosilicate_glass&action=edit&section=14 "Edit section: Rapid prototyping")]
Borosilicate glass has become the material of choice for [fused deposition modeling](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fused_deposition_modeling "Fused deposition modeling") (FDM), or fused filament fabrication (FFF), build plates.[[23]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Borosilicate_glass#cite_note-23) Its low coefficient of expansion makes borosilicate glass, when used in combination with resistance-heating plates and pads, an ideal material for the heated build platform onto which plastic materials are extruded one layer at a time.
The initial layer of build must be placed onto a substantially flat, heated surface to minimize shrinkage of some build materials ([ABS](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Acrylonitrile_butadiene_styrene "Acrylonitrile butadiene styrene"), [polycarbonate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polycarbonate "Polycarbonate"), [polyamide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polyamide "Polyamide"), etc.) due to cooling after deposition. Depending on the material used, the build plate will cycle from room temperature to between 50 and 130°C (122 and 266°F) for each prototype that is built. The temperature, along with various coatings ([Kapton tape](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kapton "Kapton"), painter's tape, hair spray, glue stick, ABS+acetone slurry, etc.), ensure that the first layer may be adhered to and remain adhered to the plate, without warping, as the first and subsequent layers cool following extrusion.
Subsequently, following the build, the heating elements and plate are allowed to cool. The resulting residual stress formed when the plastic contracts as it cools, while the glass remains relatively dimensionally unchanged due to the low coefficient of [thermal expansion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thermal_expansion "Thermal expansion"), provides a convenient aid in removing the otherwise mechanically bonded plastic from the build plate. In some cases the parts self-separate as the developed stresses overcome the adhesive bond of the build material to the coating material and underlying plate.
### Other
[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Borosilicate_glass&action=edit&section=15 "Edit section: Other")]
Aquarium heaters are sometimes made of borosilicate glass. Due to its high heat resistance, it can tolerate the significant temperature difference between the water and the [nichrome](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nichrome "Nichrome") [heating element](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heating_element "Heating element").[_[citation needed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed "Wikipedia:Citation needed")_]
Specialty glass [smoking pipes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pipe_smoking "Pipe smoking") for [cannabis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cannabis_pipe "Cannabis pipe") and [tobacco](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Smoking_pipe_\(tobacco\) "Smoking pipe \(tobacco\)") can be made from borosilicate glass. The high heat resistance makes the pipes more durable. Some [harm reduction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harm_reduction "Harm reduction") organizations also give out borosilicate pipes intended for smoking [crack cocaine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crack_cocaine "Crack cocaine"), as the heat resistance prevents the glass from cracking, causing cuts and burns that can spread [hepatitis C](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hepatitis_C "Hepatitis C").[[24]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Borosilicate_glass#cite_note-24)
[![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/2/2e/GlassGuitarSlide.jpg/250px-GlassGuitarSlide.jpg?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:GlassGuitarSlide.jpg)[Guitar slide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Slide_guitar "Slide guitar") made of borosilicate glass
Most premanufactured glass [guitar slides](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Slide_guitar "Slide guitar") are made of borosilicate glass.[_[citation needed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed "Wikipedia:Citation needed")_]
Borosilicate is also a material of choice for [evacuated-tube solar thermal technology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solar_thermal_collector#Evacuated_tube_collectors "Solar thermal collector") because of its high strength and heat resistance.[_[citation needed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed "Wikipedia:Citation needed")_]
The [thermal protection tiles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heat_shield#Spacecraft "Heat shield") on the [Space Shuttle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Space_Shuttle "Space Shuttle") and [SpaceX Starship](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SpaceX_Starship_\(spacecraft\) "SpaceX Starship \(spacecraft\)") are coated with a borosilicate glass.[[25]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Borosilicate_glass#cite_note-25)
Borosilicate glasses are used for immobilisation and disposal of [radioactive wastes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radioactive_waste "Radioactive waste"). In most countries [high-level radioactive waste](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/High-level_radioactive_waste_management "High-level radioactive waste management") has been incorporated into alkali borosilicate or phosphate vitreous waste forms for many years; [vitrification](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radioactive_waste#Vitrification "Radioactive waste") is an established technology.[[26]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Borosilicate_glass#cite_note-26)
Vitrification is a particularly attractive immobilization route because of the high chemical durability of the vitrified glass product. The chemical resistance of glass can allow it to remain in a corrosive environment for many thousands or even millions of years.
Borosilicate glass tubing is used in specialty [TIG welding](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/TIG_welding "TIG welding") torch nozzles in place of standard [alumina](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alumina "Alumina") nozzles. This allows a clear view of the arc in situations where visibility is limited.[_[citation needed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed "Wikipedia:Citation needed")_]

## Borosilicate nanoparticles
[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Borosilicate_glass&action=edit&section=17 "Edit section: Borosilicate nanoparticles")]
It was initially thought that borosilicate glass could not be formed into [nanoparticles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nanoparticle "Nanoparticle"), since an unstable [boron oxide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boron_oxide "Boron oxide") precursor would prevent successful forming of these shapes. However, in 2008 a team of researchers from the Swiss [Federal Institute of Technology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/%C3%89cole_Polytechnique_F%C3%A9d%C3%A9rale_de_Lausanne "École Polytechnique Fédérale de Lausanne") at [Lausanne](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lausanne "Lausanne") were successful in forming borosilicate nanoparticles of 100 to 500 [nanometers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nanometer "Nanometer") in diameter. The researchers formed a [gel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gel "Gel") of tetraethylorthosilicate and trimethoxyboroxine. When this gel is exposed to water under proper conditions, a dynamic reaction ensues, which results in the nanoparticles.[[27]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Borosilicate_glass#cite_note-27)
## In lampworking
[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Borosilicate_glass&action=edit&section=18 "Edit section: In lampworking")]
Borosilicate (or "boro", as it is often called) is used extensively in the [glassblowing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glassblowing "Glassblowing") process [lampworking](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lampworking "Lampworking"); the glassworker uses a burner torch to melt and form glass, using a variety of metal and [graphite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Graphite "Graphite") tools to shape it. Borosilicate is referred to as "hard glass" and has a higher melting point (approximately 3,000°F / 1,648°C) than "soft glass", which is preferred for glassblowing by beadmakers. Raw glass used in lampworking comes in glass rods for solid work and [glass tubes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glass_tubes "Glass tubes") for hollow work tubes and vessels/containers.
Lampworking is used to make complex and custom scientific apparatus; most major universities have a lampworking shop to manufacture and repair their glassware. For this kind of "scientific glassblowing", the specifications must be exact and the glassblower must be highly skilled and able to work with precision. Lampworking is also done as art, and common items made include goblets, paper weights, pipes, pendants, compositions and figurines.
In 1968, English metallurgist John Burton brought his hobby of hand-mixing metallic oxides into borosilicate glass to Los Angeles. Burton began a glass workshop at Pepperdine College, with instructor Margaret Youd. A few of the students in the classes, including Suellen Fowler, discovered that a specific combination of oxides made a glass that would shift from [amber](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shades_of_orange "Shades of orange") to purples and blues, depending on the heat and flame atmosphere.
Fowler shared this combination with Paul Trautman, who formulated the first small-batch colored borosilicate recipes. He then founded Northstar Glassworks in the mid-1980s, the first factory devoted solely to producing colored borosilicate glass rods and tubes for use by artists in the flame. Trautman also developed the techniques and technology to make the small-batch colored boro that is used by a number of similar companies.[[28]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Borosilicate_glass#cite_note-28)
### Beadmaking
[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Borosilicate_glass&action=edit&section=19 "Edit section: Beadmaking")]
In recent years[_[when?](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Manual_of_Style/Dates_and_numbers#Chronological_items "Wikipedia:Manual of Style/Dates and numbers")_], with the resurgence of lampworking as a technique to make handmade glass beads, borosilicate has become a popular material in many glass artists' studios.[_[citation needed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed "Wikipedia:Citation needed")_] Borosilicate for beadmaking comes in thin, pencil-like rods. Glass Alchemy, Trautman Art Glass, and Northstar are popular manufacturers, although there are other brands available.
The metals used to color borosilicate glass, particularly silver, often create strikingly beautiful and unpredictable results when melted in an oxygen-gas torch flame. Because it is more shock-resistant and stronger than soft glass, borosilicate is particularly suited for pipe making, as well as sculpting figures and creating large beads. The tools used for making glass beads from borosilicate glass are the same as those used for making glass beads from soft glass.
